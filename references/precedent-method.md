# Precedent Method — The Nature and Authority of Precedent

**Source:** Neil Duxbury, *The Nature and Authority of Precedent* (Cambridge University Press, 2008), supplied Markdown transcription. **Format**: md | **Pages**: main text 1–183; index through 189 | **Sections**: 5 | **Depth**: study | **Type**: text

## Mental Model (read first)

A precedent matters because an earlier decision was made, not solely because its reasoning seems correct today. Its authority can affect a later decision while leaving room for interpretation, distinction, and authorized correction. Duxbury analyzes several explanations of that authority without adopting a theory that explains it all. His philosophical account of defeasibility is not a jurisdiction-specific license to disregard superior courts.

Locations below use the book's printed pagination and chapter titles; the converter's estimate of 315 pages is not bibliographic pagination. Duxbury discusses other theorists critically. Attribution to Duxbury means his account or assessment of their views, not unqualified endorsement. The book's cases and judicial arrangements are historical illustrations, not verified contemporary case status.

## Frameworks & Structure

### 1. Introduction: the usable past — pp. 1–30

**A previous decision as a reason.** Ask whether the earlier decision itself changes the present assessment. Reaching the same result independently is not necessarily following precedent. An earlier decision can constrain even without a felt obligation if its existence changes what the decision-maker would otherwise have done.

**Precedent / analogy / rule.** These overlap without being identical. One may reason by analogy to a case without treating it as authoritative; one may follow a precedent partly because of its source rather than because its analogy is independently persuasive. A rule can have an authoritative verbal formulation which a case lacks. State what the cited case contributes: a governing proposition, authoritative reason, persuasive analogy, illustration, or historical practice.

**Precedent / habit / custom.** Repeating an act because it is routine differs from treating an identifiable past decision as a reason. Evidence of a practice can establish custom or accumulated professional understanding without amounting to a doctrine that gives individual decisions binding force. Do not erase that distinction when reading Coke or institutional precedents outside courts.

**Unintended and undiscovered precedents.** A decision may become a precedent without its maker intending it. A precedent can also apply before the present actor knows of it. Intention to set a precedent and advance knowledge are not universal conditions of precedential status. They can matter to reliance and fair notice, which require separate analysis.

**Forward-looking decisions.** Awareness that today's decision may guide future ones can influence judgment. It does not follow that every decision-maker has a general obligation to resolve tomorrow's cases too. Explore the possible reach of a proposed ruling without presenting predicted future uses as part of the existing holding.

**Classical positivism and precedent.** A sanction-backed-command model does not adequately explain the distinctive authority of a prior judicial decision. From the absence of a sanction for every departure, it does not follow that precedent has no legal force. Equally, describing precedent as binding does not explain how it binds.

**No comprehensive theory.** Do not force all precedential influence into one account—command, analogy, consent, fairness, efficiency, or exclusionary reason. Identify the mechanism that matters in the present dispute and test its limits. This methodological pluralism permits a definite answer where the materials and institutional rules support one.

### 2. Why does English law have a doctrine of precedent? — pp. 31–57

**Common law before stare decisis.** Duxbury distinguishes a system reasoning through professional learning and past materials from a developed doctrine of binding precedent. Medieval and early modern practices cannot simply be assigned a modern hierarchy of holdings. The existence of case law is not sufficient evidence for the later doctrine.

**Records, reports, abridgements, and citation.** More accessible and identifiable reports make individual decisions easier to use as authority. Changes in reporting, reference practices, and judicial organization help explain doctrinal development; they do not reduce it to a single date or technological cause. A report's availability and reliability are practical conditions for accurate use.

**The ambiguous role of classical legal positivism.** Hobbes, Bentham, and Austin seek accounts of judicial authority within broader theories of law. These are attempts to explain or criticize the practice, not a sufficient causal explanation of why it emerged. Distinguish historical formation from philosophical justification.

**Artificial reason and coherence.** The account of Coke and Hale emphasizes cultivated judgment and fit with law as a whole. Consistency with law does not necessarily mean repetition of each individual earlier judgment: a judge can have mistaken the law. Duxbury's discussion therefore does not support an absolute prohibition on correction or the claim that only a previously matched case can establish a category.

**Reasons give direction.** Earlier reasoning can shape the questions later lawyers can ask and the arguments they must answer. Precedent constrains not only outcomes but the terms of legal debate. When an argument claims novelty, check whether earlier decisions already determine the relevant issue, comparison, or burden of explanation.

### 3. Precedents as reasons — pp. 58–110

#### Three distinct roles for reasons

1. **Ratio decidendi:** the proposition or reason for the decision that helps determine the case's authority.
2. **Shortcuts to reason:** earlier work supplies reasons a later court can use, reducing or redirecting fresh deliberation.
3. **Pre-emptive or exclusionary reasons:** authority may restrict which independent reasons the later court is entitled to act on.

Do not collapse these. A well-reasoned case can be useful without controlling; a controlling case can require observance despite disagreement with its merits; finding a ratio does not establish the court's present duty to follow it.

#### Ratio decidendi and obiter dictum — pp. 67–92

**Reason for the decision / all the court's reasoning.** Not every explanatory passage forms part of a ratio. Link candidate propositions to the issues, material facts, reasoning, and disposition. Necessity is a diagnostic question, not a universal algorithm for producing one canonical rule.

**The complexity of case law.** Duxbury identifies several difficulties: ratio and dicta blur; some cases yield no identifiable common ratio; others support multiple rationes; judicial treatment may change the understood ratio; the level of generality is contestable; and a court's expression of its principle need not settle its ultimate precedential reach. Preserve competing candidates where they matter. Do not invent indeterminacy when the text and treatment are clear.

**Independent sufficient grounds.** If either of two grounds supports the result, deleting either alone leaves the other. A mechanical necessity test would classify both as dicta, although a system may treat both as authoritative. Record the grounds and the applicable treatment rule instead of using formal logic to erase them.

**Fragmented decisions.** Agreement in the disposition does not establish agreement on a proposition. Conversely, a judge dissenting from the disposition can agree on a particular issue. Map judges to issue-specific propositions and reasons before aggregating. Whether that agreement yields binding authority depends on the jurisdiction's approach; there is no universal narrowest-opinion shortcut.

**Weighty dicta.** Deliberate treatment by an authoritative court can matter greatly even when not needed to dispose of the case. Duxbury's discussion of Hedley Byrne and its reception illustrates the weakness of automatically discarding carefully considered propositions because of the disposition. Persuasive importance and technical ratio status must be kept distinct.

**Wambaugh's inversion test.** Invert a proposed rule and ask whether the decision would have changed. Use the test to expose overbroad or irrelevant formulations. Its failure on alternative sufficient grounds, multiple issues, and contested formulations means it cannot certify a ratio by itself.

**Goodhart's material-facts-and-decision approach.** Duxbury traces Goodhart's movement toward the facts the court treated as material and the conclusion reached on them, distinguishing this from treating every historical detail as relevant or equating ratio with all stated reasons. Use the approach to check an asserted holding against facts and disposition; do not adopt the inference that the judges' reasoning is irrelevant. Duxbury examines the objections rather than delivering an infallible recipe.

**Montrose and further reconstruction.** Efforts to integrate facts and reasons correct overly simple tests, but elaborate symbolic notation can increase the reader's burden without resolving the interpretive problem. Use a compact proposition map if needed; do not force all possible absent facts into a formal representation.

**Generality.** Formulate the proposition at the level supported by the court's treatment of the issue and material facts. Compare a narrower candidate with a broader one when their difference changes the answer. Narrower is not automatically more faithful: an artificially tiny holding can obscure a deliberately adopted principle.

**The point of the search.** Distinguishing ratio from dicta organizes usable legal information and limits the scope of lawmaking attributed to an opinion. Its value does not depend on every difficult case producing a uniquely correct formulation. A digest should preserve the reasons for its classification and the important unresolved alternatives.

#### Shortcuts to reason — pp. 92–99

**Piggybacking.** Earlier legal work may support both efficient and robust decisions. Identifying, comparing, and reconciling precedents can nevertheless be harder than reasoning afresh. Do not assume that following precedent is always effortless or optimally efficient.

**Accumulated authority / correlated repetition.** A line of cases may strengthen a legal position, but many citations are not necessarily independent examinations of the premise. Reliance can perpetuate a mistake or create a cascade. Check whether later courts actually considered the disputed point or merely repeated a formulation. The repository uses this as a diagnostic; raw citation counts are not a reliability score.

**Institutional reasons.** Reusing established reasoning can sustain continuity and confidence in adjudication. Those benefits do not show that every old rule is correct. Separate the reason to preserve stability from the merits of the underlying rule.

#### Pre-emptive precedent? — pp. 99–110

**First-order / second-order reasons.** A reason favoring a result differs from an authoritative reason restricting consideration of other reasons. Duxbury examines Hart's peremptory reasons and Raz's exclusionary reasons to explain why judicial disagreement on the merits need not authorize departure.

**Revisability limits the model.** Distinguishing and overruling make precedent less like a fixed verbal directive than a fully exclusionary account suggests. But criticising that account does not remove institutional constraints. The relevant inquiry remains what this court can do with this authority under the applicable practice.

**Dealing with adverse authority.** A court may need to confront a relevant prior decision even when it does not follow it. Identify and explain the route taken. “Duxbury says precedents are rebuttable” is not itself a legally sufficient route to departure.

### 4. Distinguishing, overruling and the problem of self-reference — pp. 111–149

**Distinguishing within / between cases.** Within a case, separate materially relevant propositions from other discussion. Between cases, identify a difference that changes the legal reason for the result. Every factual record differs; mere difference establishes nothing without relevance to the rule.

**Narrowing through an added condition.** Duxbury's Black/White/Grey illustration shows how requiring an additional material condition can narrow an earlier rule while preserving the prior result. Distinguishing may develop law, not merely reveal a pre-existing boundary. Ask whether the added condition is justified and whether it would leave the earlier decision standing on its facts.

**Overruling / distinguishing / reversal.** Overruling replaces an earlier rule's authority in later cases. Distinguishing preserves it while explaining non-application. Reversal concerns changing the decision in the litigation under review. Calling an act one of these does not establish that the acting court has power to perform it.

**Authority to depart.** Determine vertical and horizontal force separately. A final court's ability to reconsider its own precedents does not establish a lower court's liberty to reject a superior court. Exceptions, court composition, statutory interpretation, and current hierarchy may materially change the available route. Verify them before applying contemporary law.

**Limited correction.** Error, injustice, changing conditions, reliance, and costs of transition can bear on reconsideration where the institution permits it. If judges could replace precedent whenever their balance of reasons favored doing so, the constraint would disappear. The quality of an objection and the authority to act on it are independent questions.

**Self-binding and self-liberation.** Duxbury examines the House of Lords' earlier self-binding and the 1966 Practice Statement as changes in institutional practice. The statement's visibility helps explain how it established a durable public commitment to qualified departure. This is historical analysis, not a statement of every court's present rules.

**Constitutional propriety / logical self-reference.** Whether a court has authority to alter its practice is not settled by treating that change as an ordinary precedent which contradicts its own binding rule. Duxbury criticizes attempts to invalidate the Practice Statement through a liar-paradox analogy. Institutional authority and adoption matter; logical ingenuity alone cannot supply a doctrine of invalidity.

### 5. Why follow precedent? — pp. 150–183

**Consequentialist justifications.** Identify the proposed end and how following rather than changing a precedent serves it. Different justifications may combine; none proves that following every precedent is optimal.

| Reason offered | What must be examined | Where it can fail |
|---|---|---|
| Decisional economy | Work saved, including research and reconciliation costs | Repeating an error or litigating an unstable line can cost more |
| Predictability and planning | What actors could foresee and plan around | A predictable rule may still be seriously wrong |
| Stability and coordination | Dependencies built around the rule and transition costs | Stability can preserve avoidable error; it differs from certainty about outcomes |
| Reliance and expectations | Who actually relied, on what, and with what detriment | Reliance cannot be assumed for every old case or every litigant |
| Constraint and institutional continuity | How precedent disciplines individual choice | The same practice may protect inertia or conceal unexamined assumptions |

**Pastness as a reason.** Duxbury considers arguments that precedents ought to be followed because they are precedents, including historical continuity. That distinctive feature cannot be fully replaced by saying the earlier reasoning is good. Yet the mere fact of an earlier decision does not automatically justify obedience regardless of source, setting, or consequences.

**Formal justice: treat like cases alike.** The principle can support consistent treatment without generating a doctrine of stare decisis. A system may achieve equality without following individual prior decisions. Similarity also depends on a defended account of relevant differences; the slogan cannot choose those differences by itself.

**Permissible / mandatory difference.** A difference which permits different treatment need not require it. Identify whether a legal distinction requires, allows, or forbids the proposed response. This is particularly important when an argument concludes that two groups must be treated differently merely because the law distinguishes them in one respect.

**Consistency and error.** Equal repetition may distribute a benefit fairly, but consistent injustice is still injustice. Conversely, after one student receives information about an exam, extending it to the rest can remove an unfair advantage even if the initial disclosure was mistaken. Specify the available remedy and distributive consequences; “never repeat error” is also too simple.

**Path-dependence.** Sequence can privilege the first decision without showing it was correct. The demand for equal treatment may reflect a demand for substantive fairness, a reliance interest, or a wish to receive another person's advantage. Identify which rather than treating temporal priority as a complete justification.

**Plural support.** The conclusion is not that precedent has no authority because every argument for it is defeasible. Several limited reasons may jointly create a substantial constraint. State which reasons support following, which oppose it, and what the institution permits the decision-maker to do.

## Worked Example

Reconstruction of the distinguishing model at pp. 114–115. In Black, facts A, B, and C support outcome X; White repeats that result. In Grey, those facts are present but E is absent. A court proposes that X requires A, B, C, and E. If E identifies a legally relevant distinction and the earlier outcomes remain valid on their facts, the court has narrowed the rule while preserving those precedents. It has not merely noticed that the parties or dates differ. If E would also defeat the result in Black, the purported distinction threatens to repudiate that decision instead, requiring a different justification and possibly a different level of court. The final counterfactual is the repository's stress test of Duxbury's illustration; it is not an extra holding of an actual case.

## Decision Rules & Judgment

- When a case is cited, identify whether its contribution is authority, reasoning, analogy, practice, or evidence of a doctrinal line.
- When binding force matters, establish court, jurisdiction, hierarchy, applicable period, and treatment before discussing departure.
- When extracting a ratio, test reasons, facts, disposition, alternative grounds, and opinion alignment together.
- When a statement looks like dictum, assess its reasoned treatment and later reception rather than discarding it mechanically.
- When distinguishing, identify the additional or altered material condition and test its effect on the precedent's own result.
- When asked to overrule, separate reasons for correction from the deciding institution's power to correct.
- When many cases repeat a proposition, investigate whether they reconsidered it; repetition need not be independent confirmation.
- When equality is invoked, establish relevant likeness and defend the treatment; equality alone does not establish stare decisis.
- When all justifications have limitations, evaluate their combined force rather than concluding that no constraint exists.

## Key Takeaways

1. Precedent can constrain without behaving like an enacted verbal rule.
2. Defeasibility does not confer a general power to disregard binding authority.
3. Ratio identification is disciplined interpretation, not a mechanical necessity test.
4. Justification, scope, institutional force, and legal power to depart are different inquiries.
