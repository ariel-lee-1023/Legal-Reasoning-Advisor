## Mental Model (read first)

An argumentation scheme identifies a recurring inferential structure and the critical questions appropriate to testing it. Matching a scheme gives a provisional diagnosis, not a certificate of validity or a fallacy verdict. Determine which premise, inference, exception, or competing argument a question targets and what happens when it is answered.

This reference preserves the book's twelve-chapter structure, with operational depth on schemes most relevant to legal arguments. Chapter 9's sixty primary schemes are selectively distilled, not all reproduced. The maintainer coverage ledger identifies the retained and omitted families. ## Frameworks & Structure

### 1. Basic Tools in the State of the Art — pp. 1–42

Schemes include deductive, inductive, and defeasible forms. For a defeasible argument, its premises can support a conclusion provisionally while further information changes that support. A nondeductive form is not automatically defective; a scheme instance may still be weak because a premise lacks support or a relevant exception holds.

**Linked versus convergent support:** two linked premises work together to support one inference; convergent arguments offer separate support. If removing a premise destroys the relevance of another premise, the support may be linked. If another reason remains independently relevant, represent it as a separate route. Distinguish analytical independence from empirical independence of evidence.

**Critical questions and burdens:** questions can demand support for a premise, test applicability, raise an exception, or introduce an opposing argument. Whether merely asking shifts a burden or whether the challenger must produce support depends on the kind of premise and the dialogue. Do not use a flat rule that every unanswered question refutes an argument.

**Representation:** diagram only the contested portion. Give each premise and inference a short identifier, mark implicit premises, and connect an objection to its actual target. Araucaria illustrates the use of schemes and diagrams; the advisor does not require that software or claim a current integration.

### 2. Analogy, Classification, and Precedent — pp. 43–86

An analogy starts with a comparison case and its treatment, then proposes similar treatment for the present case. The similarity premise itself needs justification. The book's drug-sniffing-dog discussion shows why analogy can depend on a disputed classification and proposed policy rationale, rather than a simple count of shared facts.

**Operational comparison:** specify the proposition to be transferred; give the source case's treatment; list the shared features bearing on that treatment; identify the best material difference; then seek a comparably relevant case with the opposite result. Explain what principle makes a similarity or difference relevant. For institutional authority and faithful holding extraction, use [Duxbury](precedent-method.md); scheme fit does not establish binding force.

**Classification:** separate evidence that the object has property F from the rule or usage that licenses classification as G. A definition may itself be stipulated, biased, contextually unsuitable, or authoritatively enacted. The force of these objections differs. A clear governing statutory definition cannot be displaced merely by showing that ordinary speech uses another definition.

**Counterexample test:** hold the suggested reason constant while changing an apparently irrelevant detail. If the analogy ceases to persuade, identify whether an unstated reason was doing the work. Then change a legally material feature and explain why that changes the inference. This is an advisor application of the scheme and its questions, not an implementation of Ashley's HYPO.

### 3. Knowledge-Related, Practical, and Other Schemes — pp. 87–120

The book distinguishes knowledge-based inferences, practical reasoning, ignorance, consequences, fear and danger appeals, alternatives, help and excuses, composition/division, and slippery slopes. Diagnose the actual function before criticizing its surface form.

**Practical reasoning** moves from goals and means to a proposed action. Test conflicting goals, feasible alternatives, comparative effectiveness, practical possibility, and side effects. “This action helps” does not establish “this is the only permissible action.” Value-based practical reasoning adds the relationship between goals, actions, and the values invoked.

**Consequences** require both a factual or causal premise about outcomes and an evaluative premise about their desirability. An empirical uncertainty should not be answered merely by repeating the value; a value conflict should not be hidden in a forecast. Whether the relevant legal actor may use those consequences is an additional institutional question.

**Ignorance** can support an inference when a sufficiently adequate search would normally have disclosed the fact. It fails when the search domain is incomplete or detection is unreliable. Distinguish an epistemic inference from a procedural outcome caused by an unmet burden of proof.

**Composition and division:** a property of members need not belong to the group, or conversely. Apply only with a justified transmission premise. The input's compendium parentheticals for these schemes appear transposed; the advisor preserves the clear directional distinction without reproducing the damaged wording.

### 4. Generally Accepted Opinions, Commitment, and Character — pp. 121–162

Popular opinion, commitment, inconsistency, ethotic argument, circumstantial ad hominem, and bias arguments require different tests. A criticism of someone's credibility can be relevant to an argument relying on that person as a source. It is not a general refutation of every proposition they advance.

**Commitment:** identify the actual statement or action showing commitment, the interpretation attributed to it, and the inferential link to the present claim. Distinguish inconsistency in an advocate's positions from falsity of the proposition now defended. The proper repair may be withdrawal or clarification of a commitment, not a victory on the substantive issue.

**Bias:** establish the alleged bias and explain how it bears on this assertion or method. A funded expert's interest can justify investigating reliability; it does not logically prove the opinion false. Conversely, lack of a proved motive to deceive does not establish competence or evidential adequacy.

### 5. Causal Argumentation Schemes — pp. 163–188

Separate **cause to effect**, **effect to cause**, and **correlation to cause**. Each has a different vulnerability. A mechanism may be prevented from producing an effect; an observed effect may have several causes; a correlation may arise from confounding, coincidence, selection, or reverse direction.

**Use:** state the proposed cause and effect, temporal relationship, generalization, evidence for it, and relevant interfering or alternative factors. Identify an observation that would discriminate between the favored explanation and a live alternative. A list of possible causes without a connection to the evidence is not a completed causal analysis.

### 6. Schemes and Enthymemes — pp. 189–219

An **enthymeme**, in the usage examined here, has an unstated premise or conclusion. Scheme matching helps generate plausible candidates, but attribution remains constrained by what the speaker said and the dialogue's commitments. Inserting a premise that makes an argument deductively valid can misrepresent a defeasible argument or repair an argument the speaker did not make.

**Procedure:** retain the supplied claim; identify the missing inferential function; propose the least committal plausible premise sufficient for that function; mark it as reconstructed; check textual and contextual fit; then assess its support. If an improved alternative is stronger but unsupported as an attribution, label it a proposed repair. Avoid both hostile straw-man reconstruction and charitable invention of evidence.

### 7. Attack, Opposition, Rebuttal, and Refutation — pp. 220–274

Distinguish challenging an argument from successfully defeating it. Contradictory conclusions, contrary conclusions, criticism of a source, and criticism of an inferential step are different relations. Internal criticism can expose a conflict within the proponent's commitments; external criticism can contest those commitments with additional grounds.

**Objection record, advisor adaptation:** record target; kind of challenge; supporting reason or evidence; who must answer; the best available answer; resulting effect. Use outcomes such as “premise still unsupported,” “exception established for this route,” or “question answered; support retained.” “Question asked” is not an outcome assessment.

For technical defeat and reinstatement, load [Prakken](reference-prakken-defeasible-argument.md). The meanings of rebuttal and attack vary across models; do not silently identify every use in this compendium with one formal definition.

### 8. The History of Schemes — pp. 275–307

The authors trace connections to topics in Aristotle, Cicero, Boethius, medieval and Renaissance traditions, and modern argumentation theory. Their classification proposals address different levels: the type of conclusion, a scheme's support or refutation role, and its place in discourse. These are not one exhaustive mutually exclusive ontology.

**Use:** keep source, function, and logical form separate when comparing taxonomies. The historical genealogy is retained as a limit on claims of novelty and universality; this module does not reproduce the full history of topics or each historical taxonomy.

The following cards paraphrase the selected schemes and critical questions, adding clearly practical legal repair steps. Numbers identify the compendium's primary entries, not an advisor priority ranking.

| 1. Position to know | A source with access asserts P, provisionally supporting P | Was the source in a position to know? Trustworthy here? Did it actually assert P? | Obtain the original assertion and basis of access; narrow the claim to what was observed |

These are focused tests, not a requirement to ask every question on every case. Expand only the inference that controls the answer. For evidence combinations, use the fuller chart method rather than attempting to derive aggregate probative weight from scheme counts.

**A distinctive compendium use of precedent (57):** this entry starts with a generally established rule and a legitimate case apparently violating it, then argues for recognizing or modifying an exception. It is not merely the positive similarity-transfer scheme (7). Test whether the rule really applies, whether the apparent counterexample is legitimate, and whether an existing exception already explains it. The broader legal status of that case still needs the institutional analysis in Duxbury.

**Constitutive rules and excuse (54, 58):** an official rulebook's statement of what counts as an institutional act depends on its currency and authority. An excuse can address sanction despite admitting the act; it need not deny the act or establish its legality. Both are different from merely asking whether an established rule's conditions hold.

### 10. Refining the Classification of Schemes — pp. 347–363

The compendium and the finer treatment of ad hominem reveal overlapping schemes and subschemes. Source-based arguments, applying rules to cases, and practical reasoning are useful families, but an actual paragraph may instantiate several. Assign a scheme to an inferential step, not to the whole document or a person.

### 11. Formalizing Schemes — pp. 364–392

The discussion covers defeasible modus ponens, representations such as AML and AIF, Carneades, and dialogue systems. Formalization requires more than putting premises in boxes. In particular, ordinary premises, assumptions, and exceptions can have different burdens of support in a dialogue; there is no single effect shared by all critical questions.

**Advisor use:** make these roles explicit when they affect an objection. Do not claim that an informal table runs Carneades or reproduces its proof standards. A software implementation would require an explicit semantics and separate verification; the supplied OCR is not a reliable basis for transcribing executable syntax.

### 12. Schemes in Computer Systems — pp. 393–414

The book surveys diagramming, natural-language analysis, interagent communication, and automated reasoning applications. These exemplify representation and support for argumentation, not evidence that contemporary language models reliably apply the schemes. Using a scheme library is a design intervention whose behavioral effect must be tested.

## Worked Example

### Source reconstruction: corporate income tax — ch. 6, pp. 199–201

The book analyzes an argument for abolishing corporate income tax because it encourages waste and high prices. It reconstructs the consequences bridge and the evaluation of waste and high prices. The argument is better treated as defeasible than forced into “every measure with any bad consequence must be abolished.”

**Test:** establish the asserted causal effects; identify benefits or competing consequences; compare feasible alternatives to abolition. A valid criticism may weaken the recommended policy without denying that a particular bad effect exists. These are illustrations of inferential roles, not this advisor's fiscal advice.

### Constructed expert argument and revision

“A respected bridge engineer says the signature on this contract is forged, so the agreement is void.”

1. **Expert-opinion step:** respect and engineering expertise do not establish relevant expertise in signature authentication. Identify the engineer's actual basis: direct observation of signing, specialist analysis, or speculation. These support different schemes.
2. **Classification step:** “forged” may contain an unproved factual and legal characterization. Determine what the report actually establishes.
3. **Legal consequence step:** even an established unauthorized signature requires a supplied legal rule connecting it to the claimed effect. Ratification, other manifestations of agreement, or the actor's role may matter if supported by the applicable materials.
4. **Answer to the challenge:** if a properly qualified examiner supplies a supported opinion, the original field objection is answered for that new evidence. Reassess its method and scope; do not repeat the defeated objection.
5. **Counterexample:** the engineer personally watched the signature being fabricated. The relevant initial support may now be eyewitness testimony rather than specialist expertise. Criticizing the engineering credential alone misses the strongest actual argument.
6. **Revision:** narrow the conclusion to what the evidence supports; assess the legal effect separately. If a supplied ratification provision applies, signature falsity and effectiveness may coexist. A factual dispute and its remedial consequence require separate routes.

## Decision Rules & Judgment

- Choose a scheme because its inferential function fits; identify the material alternative classification if uncertain.
- Attach each critical question to a premise, applicability condition, exception, or rival argument, and state its present answer or evidential gap.
- A relevant credibility challenge is not automatically ad hominem fallacy; an established credibility defect is not automatically falsity of the conclusion.
- Treat reconstructed premises as reconstructions, not admissions. Label improvements as repairs.
- After a supported reply, update the objection's status. Do not leave every originally raised challenge permanently active.
- Use scheme-specific questions to obtain a discriminating next step, not a long generic checklist.

## Key Takeaways

Schemes make criticism more precise when paired with their critical questions, dialogue roles, and burden distinctions. Their main contribution is targeted testing of an inference. Scheme matching, asking a question, and successful refutation are three different achievements.
