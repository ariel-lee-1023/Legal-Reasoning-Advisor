# Analysis of Evidence — Terence Anderson, David Schum, and William Twining

**Source:** Terence Anderson, David Schum, and William Twining, *Analysis of Evidence*, second edition (Cambridge University Press, 2005), supplied Markdown transcription. **Format**: md | **Pages**: chapters pp. 1–378; glossary 379, references 388, index 396 onward | **Sections**: 12 | **Depth**: study | **Type**: text

## Mental Model (read first)

Evidence of an event is not the event. Build the inferential path from an identifiable evidential item to an asserted event, through intermediate propositions, to the legally material facts. Expose the generalization supporting each consequential step and the opponent's strongest explanation, denial, or rival. A coherent story is useful for organizing the case; its missing links cannot be supplied by narrative fluency.

The central operational method is the authors' adaptation of Wigmore's chart method, complemented by outlines, chronologies, stories, and explicit evaluation. The compact textual chart used below adapts its representation, not its proof discipline. This reference treats historical cases and evidentiary rules as illustrations. It does not reproduce separately hosted online appendices or establish current admissibility law.

## Frameworks & Structure

### 1. Evidence and Inference: Some Food for Thought — pp. 1–45

The exercises expose the distance between a plausible account and justified proof. The same materials can be approached as investigator, advocate, fact-finder, historian, or reviewer. Fix the standpoint, question, available record, and purpose before assessing a claim.

**Use:** distinguish “what happened?”, “what does this record support?”, “what may be admitted?”, and “what decision is authorized at this procedural stage?” An answer to one does not automatically answer the others. A motion assuming pleaded facts differs from a trial evaluating whether those facts are proved.

### 2. Fact Investigation and the Nature of Evidence — pp. 46–77

**E* and E:** E* is the evidence or report of an event; E is the event said to have occurred. First ask what an exhibit or witness actually establishes. A photograph's existence does not automatically establish its date, location, or authenticity. A witness may report a direct observation, repeat another person's report, or infer a conclusion from observations. Trace the difference.

**Levels of probanda:** the **ultimate probandum** is the factual proposition or compound of propositions required by the governing rule; **penultimate probanda** are its material components; **interim probanda** are the intermediate facts inferred along the way. Do not label the ultimate factual compound “the law” or treat legal entitlement as one observed fact. If the governing interpretation is unsettled, create alternative material-fact structures rather than hiding the issue.

**Three credentials of evidence:**

- **Relevance:** identify the particular proposition the evidence tends to support or undermine and the inferential path to a material fact.
- **Credibility:** assess whether the report or item is reliable for this use.
- **Probative force / weight:** assess how strongly it favors the proposition against the relevant alternative. Relevance alone is not sufficiency.

**Testimonial credibility:** **veracity** concerns conformity to the witness's beliefs; **objectivity** concerns forming those beliefs from evidence rather than wishes or expectations; **observational sensitivity** concerns the quality and conditions of perception. A sincere witness may be mistaken. Poor lighting challenges sensitivity, not necessarily honesty. Ancillary evidence about recall, bias, opportunity, conditions, and prior statements must be connected to the appropriate attribute and occasion.

**Tangible evidence:** examine authenticity, continuity of custody or provenance, accuracy and sensitivity of the recording process, and whether a representation has been mistaken for what it represents. Competence to testify and credibility are separate. Hearsay may be useful as an investigative lead even if its trial use would face a legal restriction; preserve that distinction.

### 3. Principles of Proof — pp. 78–111

The **Rationalist Tradition** makes factual accuracy, reasoned inference, and justified decision central while recognizing institutional constraints. The book uses that tradition as an analytical foundation; it does not eliminate disputes over its assumptions or the values served by procedure.

**Probative processes:**

| Process | Role in the dispute | Response required |
|---|---|---|
| Proponent's assertion (PA) | Evidence supports a proposed inference | State the evidence, inference, and generalization |
| Opponent's denial (OD) | Denies an asserted proposition | Distinguish a bare challenge from evidence supporting the denial |
| Opponent's rival (OR) | Supplies an incompatible affirmative account | Trace its evidence and compare the conflicting propositions |
| Opponent's explanation (OE) | Accounts for the evidence while weakening the proposed inference | Identify whether context already supports the alternative or whether additional evidence is needed |
| Proponent's corroboration (PC) | Strengthens the assertion against the challenge | Explain which doubt the added support answers |

**Conjunction:** if a conclusion requires A and B, support for A does not fill a gap in B. “Analytically distinct elements” does not mean statistically independent variables. Do not multiply assumed probabilities merely because a legal rule has multiple elements.

**Convergence:** distinct facts can independently bear on one conclusion—for example, motive and opportunity on identity. **Corroboration**, in the narrower usage adopted for this analysis, concerns independent testimony to the same proposition. Test common information sources, collusion, shared perceptual conditions, and dependence before treating repeated accounts as added independent support.

**Catenate inference:** a chain E* → E → F → a material proposition introduces a possible uncertainty at each step. Show the missing link that matters; avoid adding nodes merely to make a diagram look rigorous. Multiple independent sufficient arguments differ from a single conjunction or chain. Their aggregate force cannot be read from the number of arrows.

**Generalizations:** these license the inferential links and are commonly qualified. The authors themselves differ on whether every inference requires a generalization: Anderson and Schum favor “all”; Twining uses the more cautious “certainly most.” The advisor requires consequential inferred transitions to be made assessable without claiming this philosophical disagreement is settled.

### 4. Methods of Analysis — pp. 112–122

The book distinguishes charts, outlines, and narrative methods with complementary devices. Choose based on the analytical problem. An outline may suffice for a small argument; a chart helps when dependencies and opposition become hard to track; a chronology aligns times without proving causation.

The seven-step protocol provides a revisable discipline, not a one-pass algorithm. Change a provisional theory when the evidence or the governing interpretation changes. The objective is a better analysis, not completion of a form.

### 5. The Chart Method — pp. 123–144

**Seven-step protocol, paraphrased from pp. 124–133:**

1. **Clarify standpoint:** analyst's role, procedural stage, available materials, and purpose.
2. **Formulate the ultimate probandum:** derive the needed factual compound from the applicable legal rule; preserve uncertainty about that rule.
3. **Formulate potential penultimate probanda:** separate required elements and strategically significant subsidiary facts.
4. **Formulate provisional theories for both sides:** articulate the strongest plausible competing accounts, including alternative interpretations if needed.
5. **Formulate the key-list:** number simple, precise propositions; include source reports, material and intermediate facts, generalizations, and opposing propositions. Link evidential entries to their source locations.
6. **Prepare charts:** work down from the material facts and up from evidence; group difficult portions into sectors; connect support and opposition to exact targets. The same item may bear on more than one issue but remains one item.
7. **Refine and complete:** check every consequential inferential gap, counterargument, circular relationship, and omission; revise the key-list, charts, theories, and priorities together.

**Compact representation, advisor adaptation:** use `E1 [source report] -> F1 [event] --G1 [generalization]--> I1 [interim fact] -> P1 [material fact]`. Add `O1 undermines G1` or `R1 supports not-I1` where relevant. Mark a conjunction as `P1 AND P2`; give independent routes separate IDs. Arrows mean inferential support, not temporal succession or proven causation.

**Chart quality checks:** every important evidence entry has provenance; every disputed inference has a stated bridge; opposing evidence is located rather than appended at the end; no conclusion is its own evidential support; reused evidence is not double-counted; chronology and argument structure are distinguishable. A chart remains the analyst's reasoned construction, not an objective machine verdict.

### 6. Outlines, Chronologies, and Narrative — pp. 145–158

An outline organizes the case by issues and subissues. A chronology orders events. A narrative gives an intelligible account. A theory of the case connects the legal position to a supported factual account; a theme emphasizes a persuasive idea. These artifacts can inform each other without becoming interchangeable.

**Use:** start with an issue outline, build a chronology only when timing matters, and chart a disputed causal or evidential segment. Revise presentation for pleading, investigation, or trial preparation while preserving the distinction between assumed and proved facts. A theme must not smuggle an allegation into the evidential record.

### 7. Analyzing the Decided Case: Anatomy of a Cause Célèbre — pp. 159–223

The Bywaters and Thompson materials permit reconstruction of evidence, alternative interpretations, and the role of prejudicial generalizations. Letters can establish words written without conclusively establishing that described acts occurred, that proposals were acted upon, or that a particular mental state existed at the legally relevant moment.

**Operation preserved:** distinguish a statement's content from the inference it supports; place letters and testimony in temporal context; compare alternative readings; identify the additional bridge from fantasy, hostility, or relationship to participation in the charged act. The module does not supply a fresh verdict on the complete historical case or reproduce the extensive exhibits.

### 8. Evaluating Evidence — pp. 224–245

Evaluation depends on the decision at issue. Investigation, advice, admissibility, determination as a matter of law, verdict, and appellate review have different questions and standards. An advocate deciding whether to investigate a lead need not already possess evidence sufficient for a verdict.

**Use:** identify burden bearer, burden type where relevant, issue, stage, and applicable standard. Assess each material proposition and the overall conclusion under that standard. A civil or criminal label alone is insufficient where the actual question concerns admissibility or an interlocutory motion. Do not silently replace a legal standard with a personal confidence percentage.

**Result distinction:** “the burden was not met” does not necessarily mean “the contrary event occurred.” Conversely, a factual inference can be strong without resolving whether an item is legally admissible or a remedy available.

### 9. Probabilities, Weight, and Probative Force — pp. 246–261

The book compares four conceptions rather than prescribing a universal scoring scheme:

- **Conventional probability and Bayes:** distinguish prior belief, likelihood of evidence under hypotheses, and posterior belief. Evidential force involves comparing `P(E | H)` with `P(E | alternative)`, not equating either with `P(H | E)`. Combining evidence requires attention to dependence and the hypothesis actually at issue.
- **Shafer's evidential support:** lack of support and disbelief are different; the representation permits uncommitted support. Its combination rule and quantities are not interchangeable with Bayesian probabilities.
- **Baconian probability:** examine evidential coverage and recognized tests the favored hypothesis has survived. A large amount of confirming material can coexist with decisive questions left unanswered. Do not turn the number of unanswered questions into an invented probability.
- **Wigmore and fuzzy weight:** qualitative judgments of inferential force can be useful when numerical assignment lacks a basis. The chapter discusses fuzzy methods, but ordinary verbal strengths are not a validated aggregate scoring algorithm.

**Sacco illustration, pp. 251–253:** even evidence that the fatal bullet came from a particular gun leaves the identity of the shooter to be inferred. It also raises authenticity and comparison reliability issues. The relevant likelihood comparison depends on the proposition under examination; a match statistic is not a guilt probability.

**Calculation discipline, advisor application:** calculate only with supplied or justified quantities and explicit conditioning assumptions. Otherwise explain direction of support, important dependencies, and missing information qualitatively. The online probability appendix is referenced by the source but was not part of the supplied text as a separately verified appendix.

### 10. Necessary but Dangerous: Generalizations and Stories — pp. 262–288

Generalizations may be case-specific, scientific, matters of general knowledge, experience-based, or synthetic-intuitive beliefs. Identify which sort is doing the work and its degree of qualification. “Common sense” can hide an untested stereotype. The apparent plausibility of a story can derive from prejudices rather than evidence.

**Story-testing protocol, condensed from pp. 280–282:** articulate the full story; test internal consistency; locate contrary evidence; identify support for each material part; isolate speculation; check for irrelevant, invented, or inadmissible material; examine emotional language, implicit character judgments, and competing stories. Evaluate the strongest alternative against the same record and standards.

**Revision:** remove an unsupported event and determine which downstream inferences actually fail. If the story remains vivid but loses its only evidence for an element, change the assessment. If new evidence answers a particular uncertainty, update that link without pretending it settles unrelated doubts.

### 11. Principles of Proof and the Law of Evidence — pp. 289–314

Logical relevance and evidential reasoning interact with legal admissibility rules but are not identical to them. Identify the purpose for which an item is offered and its chain to a material issue; only then analyze the applicable exclusion, exception, foundation, or balancing rule.

**Use:** the same statement may be offered to prove its content, notice, the speaker's state of mind, or another proposition. These are different uses with different inferential and legal questions. Label them rather than broadly declaring “hearsay” or “admissible.” Verify current primary rules when answering a practical legal question.

### 12. The Trial Lawyer's Standpoint — pp. 315–378

The culminating exercises apply the methods to preparation and advocacy. A lawyer selects and presents evidence with a purpose while needing a serious account of the opponent's case. The strongest factual theory may still be procedurally unavailable or inadequately supported for trial.

**Use:** organize investigation around discriminating facts, compare what each witness can actually establish, and prepare for the strongest challenge to decisive links. Keep the client recommendation tied to what can be established and what remains unknown. This module preserves the method, not solutions to every exercise or a complete trial-practice manual.

## Worked Example

### Source argument reconstructed: United States v. Able — ch. 5, pp. 125–133

The case exercise contrasts intentional underreporting with disclosure to and reliance on an accountant. A bank endorsement from Las Vegas is offered along a motive chain: money was received there; it was gambled; it was lost; the loss created pressure; that pressure supplied a motive to underreport. The chain becomes questionable only when those intermediate steps are stated.

**Opponent's explanation:** receipt in Las Vegas does not establish gambling; gambling does not establish substantial loss; loss does not establish intentional false filing. Alternative uses, winnings, or limited losses challenge different links. The fact of underreporting remains distinct from knowledge and intent.

**Revision:** obtain evidence about the missing transitions and the communications to the accountant. Proof of disclosure and reliance bears on knowledge or intent; it need not dispute the return's contents. A new gambling record can support one link without curing the independent gap concerning what the accountant was told. The source example teaches investigation through the chart, not a supplied verdict.

### Constructed evidence chart: an allegedly altered inspection record

Three employees say a safety entry was added after an accident. E1 is the first employee's recollection of the screen; E2 and E3 repeat what that employee told them. E4 is an export showing the entry's current content. The claim is that a manager knowingly falsified the record before an investigation.

**Key-list:** F1: the first employee saw no entry at time t1; I1: no entry existed at t1; F2: the current record contains the entry; I2: someone added it after t1; P1: the manager added it; P2: the entry was false; P3: the manager knew that; P4: the act preceded the relevant investigation. The governing offense or sanction rule, if absent, remains an additional legal gap.

**Support and opposition:** E1 may support F1, depending on perception and recall. The transition to I1 requires that the screen was complete and showed the correct record. E2/E3 establish repetition, not two new observations. E4 establishes present content subject to provenance; it does not identify author or edit time. Even I2 would not establish P1–P3 without further support. An authorized late correction is a rival explanation, not a found fact.

**Discriminating evidence:** an authenticated audit trail with account attribution, timestamp semantics, and change history would address when and by whom an entry changed. It would not alone establish falsity or knowledge. Contemporaneous inspection notes address content and knowledge separately.

**Update:** if the trail establishes a post-accident edit by the manager, update authorship and timing. Keep falsity and knowledge unresolved unless supported. If evidence establishes an accurate authorized correction, the mere fact of a late edit no longer warrants “falsification.” Neither result licenses a blanket declaration about every allegation in the dispute.

## Decision Rules & Judgment

- Start with standpoint and the material facts required, then connect evidence to them. Do not begin by choosing the most compelling story.
- Distinguish E* from E at the first consequential inference. Identify observation, repetition, inference, and documentary provenance.
- Separate relevance, credibility, probative force, admissibility, and sufficiency. Success on one does not establish the others.
- Mark conjunctions, chains, convergence, and corroboration; preserve common-source dependencies wherever evidence is reused.
- Target the strongest live rival, not every imaginable story. State what observation would discriminate and whether it can still be obtained.
- Revisit the whole argument after a change. Withdraw failed downstream inferences; preserve supported independent routes; reassess the actual proof standard.

## Key Takeaways

The deliverable is an inspectable connection between evidence and material facts, with exact doubts and opposing explanations. Charts discipline that work; they do not establish facts or calculate certainty by themselves. A story's plausibility, the evidence's force, and legal sufficiency must each be assessed.
