# Rhetoric and the Rule of Law — Neil MacCormick

**Source:** Neil MacCormick, *Rhetoric and the Rule of Law: A Theory of Legal Reasoning* (Oxford University Press, 2005), supplied Markdown transcription. **Format**: md | **Pages**: main text 1–280; indexes through 287 | **Sections**: 13 | **Depth**: study | **Type**: text

## Mental Model (read first)

Legal justification has a deductive structure without being exhausted by deduction. A conclusion may follow from stated premises while the choice, interpretation, proof, or application of those premises remains contested. Find which dispute remains. Then supply the kind of reason capable of resolving it. Persuasion here is reasoned justification addressed to others within legal institutions, not success at making an audience feel convinced.

MacCormick's organizing problem is how an arguable law can sustain the Rule of Law. Universalizable reasons, institutional sources, and reasoned correction make contestability compatible with qualified certainty. They do not promise mechanical answers or unrestricted discretion. The procedural sequence below is this advisor's operational adaptation of the book; its classifications and distinctions retain the author's terminology. Historical statutes and judgments illustrate reasoning, not current law.

## Frameworks & Structure

### 1. Prologue: Institutional Theory and the Lawmaker's Perspective — pp. 1–11

**Institutional facts.** Legal acts occur within institutions constituted and regulated by norms. A physical description of a signature or payment does not alone tell us whether there was a contract, valid exercise of power, or discharge. State the constitutive conditions and the relevant actor's competence. This provides a bridge to Hohfeld's relations without treating relational classification as a source of law.

**Lawmaker's perspective.** A norm commonly associates operative facts with normative consequences. Start with the contemplated consequence and identify its conditions. Check whether the enactment supplies an obligation, a power, a discretion, or a rule constituting an institution. Do not convert every provision into an unconditional duty or treat every consequence as punishment.

### 2. The Rule of Law and the Arguable Character of Law — pp. 12–31

The two commonplaces are that people need law to guide conduct and that legal propositions can be argued over. MacCormick examines rhetorical and proceduralist approaches to their reconciliation. Legal argument is constrained by public reasons and institutional materials even where more than one position is initially plausible.

**Use:** identify the reasonably settled starting points before opening contested ones. The existence of disagreement does not make every position equally supportable. Conversely, an authoritative outcome does not show that its reasons are beyond criticism. Fair procedures help secure legitimate decisions; assess the reasons themselves as well as the procedure.

### 3. On the Legal Syllogism — pp. 32–48

The basic shape is: when the operative conditions hold, a normative consequence follows; these conditions hold here; therefore this consequence follows. Real arguments often require several linked syllogisms and distinguish substantive legal consequences from the judgment an institution should issue.

**Make the inference inspectable:**

1. Name the consequence precisely: damages, invalidity, a duty to decide, or permission to exercise discretion.
2. State the legal conditional with its material conditions, actors, time, and exceptions.
3. Match each required condition to a factual or institutional proposition.
4. Check that the claimed result has the same scope as the consequent. A power to award relief is not a duty to award a particular sum.
5. Separately justify the conditional and its application. Writing it down has not established it.

**Dignity Funerals illustration, ch. 3, pp. 32–33:** MacCormick distinguishes whether dismissal caused the relevant wage loss from what compensation would be just and equitable. A favorable causal finding opens a discretionary question; it does not calculate the award. This is the source of the two-stage diagnosis, not a present employment-law rule.

### 4. Defending Deductivism — pp. 49–77

MacCormick answers objections about gaps, norms and truth values, institutional facts, classification, proof procedures, evaluations, and normative predicates. Deductive organization does not assume that law is gapless, that rules select themselves, or that disputed classification is a mere observation.

**Four locations of difficulty:** proof asks whether a factual proposition is established; interpretation asks what an authoritative text means; classification asks whether the circumstances fall within a legal category; relevancy asks whether a proposed rule or ground can legally justify the result. These difficulties can overlap. “Is this loss?” may combine a dispute about the meaning of a statutory term with one about what actually occurred. Keep the alternative answers separate until their dependencies are clear.

**Daniels v White and Tarbard, pp. 49–50:** the purchaser's contractual claim against the seller and negligence claims against the manufacturer had different conditions. Lack of proved manufacturing negligence did not defeat the contractual route; the purchaser's wife did not acquire that contractual route just because she suffered similar injury. The lesson is to preserve parties and legal grounds, not to generalize the historical outcome to modern product liability.

**Failure:** concluding that syllogistic organization is useless because a premise is contestable, or that a valid syllogism ends the inquiry because its contentious premise has been hidden in the major premise.

### 5. Universals and Particulars — pp. 78–100

**Universalization versus generalization.** A reason that justifies a result must be usable for materially similar cases. That normative commitment differs from predicting, by induction, that most cases will have the same result. Universalizability does not require an exceptionless rule; countervailing circumstances can qualify the reason.

**Boundary procedure:** formulate the reason in terms that omit party names; replace an irrelevant detail; ask whether the same reason still applies. Then introduce a plausibly material circumstance and explain why it qualifies the reason. Refine the rule openly instead of defining it as “all and only cases in which my preferred party should win.”

The Solomon discussion exposes both the power and limits of a particular judgment: the supposed reason for restoring a child to its mother is generalizable as a reason, while adoption, incapacity, and other relationships complicate its application. It is a philosophical example, not a custody test or endorsement of its factual psychology.

### 6. Judging by Consequences — pp. 101–120

**Juridical consequences** are implications of adopting a ruling for other legally relevant situations. Distinguish them from uncertain causal predictions about what people will do after judgment. Both may matter, but their support differs.

**Second-order justification:** test a proposed ruling for consistency with established law, coherence with relevant principles and analogies, and the acceptability of its consequences in light of legal values. These are argumentative constraints, not three numbers to add. A proposal may survive one test and fail another. Explain the conflict rather than counting favorable considerations.

In the book's discussion of *Dudley and Stephens*, the necessity defense is tested through what its proposed formulation would authorize in other emergencies. That juridical implication differs from an empirical claim about how many future killings the decision will cause. Personal hardship and the merits of mitigation are also distinct from whether the proposed legal defense is justified.

**Revision:** narrow an overbroad proposed exception only if the narrower condition has a legal reason and preserves justified cases. If the objection is causal, investigate the causal premise. If it is lack of institutional competence, a better prediction alone cannot repair it.

### 7. Arguing about Interpretation — pp. 121–142

The book distinguishes **linguistic**, **systemic**, and **teleological-evaluative** arguments; intention can enter these in different senses rather than functioning as a self-explanatory fourth trump.

| Type | Operation | What could defeat it |
|---|---|---|
| Ordinary or technical meaning | Identify the relevant register, sentence, and context; articulate competing readings | A technical definition, surrounding provision, or established usage inconsistent with the suggested reading |
| Contextual harmonization | Read the provision within the enactment and related legislation | A proposed harmony that erases an express difference or exception |
| Precedent | Identify a prior interpretation and its legal force | Different issue, later treatment, or lack of authority in the present forum |
| Analogy | Compare another provision and explain the legally material similarity | Different purpose, structure, or express allocation of consequences |
| Conceptual argument | Test consistency of a legally developed concept within its relevant field | A special statutory use or a demonstrated reason for different treatment |
| General principles | Identify the principle and show its bearing on competing readings | Competing principles or authoritative limitations left unanswered |
| History | Explain continuity or change against the relevant historical legal setting | Treating historical use as controlling despite a material legislative change |
| Teleological-evaluative argument | State the supported end or value and compare how each reading serves it | An invented purpose, unsupported consequence, or impermissible alteration of scope |

Distinguish objective purpose attributed to a text from a particular lawmaker's subjective intention. Establish the admissibility and weight of interpretive materials under the applicable system. MacCormick discusses a tendency to begin with linguistic and systemic arguments and move to further evaluation when problems remain; this is not a universal hierarchy binding every jurisdiction.

**Dunnachie, chs. 7–8:** whether statutory “loss” encompasses nonpecuniary injury cannot be decided merely by choosing the widest dictionary meaning. Related provisions, earlier legislation, prior decisions, and the place of discretion constrain competing readings. Record this as the book's historical interpretation example, not a current claim about recoverable loss.

### 8. Using Precedents — pp. 143–162

A **ratio decidendi** connects the disposition to a ruling on a disputed legal issue. Reconstructing reasons charitably is useful, but an analyst's most rational argument may differ from the court's actual argument. Label that difference.

**Use:** identify the claim, issue, operative facts, candidate ruling, reasoning for the ruling, and outcome. Compare an actual reconstruction with a proposed improvement. For binding force, fragmented opinions, independent grounds, and authorized departure, load [Duxbury](precedent-method.md). MacCormick's account of justification and Duxbury's inquiry into authority answer related but different questions; neither eliminates the institutional inquiry.

### 9. Being Reasonable — pp. 163–188

Reasonableness is indexed to its subject, legally relevant factors, values, and the role of the decision-maker. Identifying the factors is itself often a legal interpretive question. Evaluating their significance in a particular case may require judgment that cannot be reduced to a definition.

**Use:** specify what is to be reasonable—conduct, an official decision, reliance, doubt—and for whom, with what information at what time. Identify which factors the governing standard permits or requires. Then assess the balance with reasons. Do not transfer an appellate restraint standard to the initial merits decision, or treat “reasonable” as a license to apply personal preferences.

### 10. Coherence, Principles, and Analogies — pp. 189–212

**Consistency** excludes contradiction; **normative coherence** concerns intelligibility through values or principles. A set of different speed limits by car color can be consistent yet appear arbitrary. Add an actual relationship between color coding, vehicle characteristics, and safety, and an initially arbitrary distinction may become intelligible. The change must be supported, not invented to rescue a result.

**Analogy and principle:** explain why the shared characteristic matters through a principle that makes sense of the source cases or rules. List a relevant disanalogy and test whether it alters that principle's application. Identical vocabulary and a similar narrative do not suffice. Coherence can guide development while leaving multiple defensible extensions; it is not automatic proof of unique correctness.

### 11. Legal Narratives — pp. 213–236

Narratives situate events in time and can connect them intelligibly. A legal narrative must also establish legally relevant propositions through acceptable evidence. **Narrative coherence** and **normative coherence** are distinct: one makes a sequence of alleged events intelligible; the other makes norms intelligible through principles and values.

In the washhouse litigation discussed at pp. 221–222, an accident's occurrence did not rescue a pleaded mechanical explanation that the evidence failed to support. Formulating an alternative causal story also raised procedural questions. Use this example to distinguish event, causal account, pleaded case, and permitted revision. Load [Analysis of Evidence](reference-analysis-of-evidence.md) for the actual evidence-to-probandum method.

### 12. Arguing Defeasibly — pp. 237–253

An initially sufficient-looking claim can face a legally relevant exception. MacCormick favors examining the pragmatic defeat of claims and formulations without settling every ontological dispute about whether rules themselves are intrinsically defeasible. Exceptions do not make rules optional.

**Use:** identify the claim supported so far; articulate the proposed exception; establish its facts and legal relevance; answer it; and revise the formulation if warranted. Keep the power to recognize an exception distinct from its attractiveness. Universalizable rulings remain open to counterexamples and rephrasing; no finite checklist proves that every future exception has been exhausted.

### 13. Judging Mistakenly? — pp. 254–280

Judicial finality and judicial correctness are different. Institutional settlement can coexist with reasoned criticism and authorized correction. A claim that a judge erred requires showing which legal or factual reason fails; disagreement or adverse consequences alone do not establish error.

**Use:** distinguish “this outcome governs the parties,” “this ruling binds this court,” and “this reasoning is the best justified.” Identify the available procedure for correction separately from the criticism. Do not infer a reviewing institution's power merely from the strength of the criticism.

## Worked Example

**Constructed application; no real jurisdiction assumed.** A supplied rule says a panel may award fair compensation for documented loss caused by a cancellation. A claimant produces a canceled booking and says: “Cancellation is established, therefore the panel must award my entire demand.”

1. **Inference:** the rule's consequent is discretionary compensation for a specified kind of loss; the claim demands a particular mandatory amount. Even assuming cancellation, the inference exceeds the premise.
2. **Proof:** identify the alleged loss and evidence linking it to cancellation. The booking proves neither expenditure nor inability to obtain a refund.
3. **Interpretation:** if “loss” is disputed, state competing readings and inspect the supplied definition, surrounding provisions, and authorized sources. Do not silently choose the claimant's interpretation.
4. **Classification:** determine whether each proved item falls within the justified reading. Distinguish a documented fee from asserted disappointment.
5. **Relevancy and remedy:** identify why this claimant and transaction fall within the rule and why this panel can award the relief.
6. **Counterargument:** a refund record may defeat one alleged loss while leaving another expense supported. It does not prove that every possible award is unavailable.
7. **Revision:** with the fee established and no refund, the claim becomes an argument for compensation within discretion. A supplied mandatory tariff could change the remedial inference, but would need its own applicability analysis.

**Boundary check:** change the claimant's identity or the booking's medium while holding legally relevant conditions fixed. If the conclusion changes, articulate a legal reason for that change. Then change the operative fact—full reimbursement—and explain which compensation route fails. This is rule testing, not a numerical forecast of judicial behavior.

## Decision Rules & Judgment

- If the conclusion does not follow even on the assumed premises, repair the inference before debating policy.
- If it does follow, identify the disputed premise and the right mode of justification. Avoid generic demands for “more evidence” when the missing support is legal authority.
- If a reason justifies only this named party, test its universalizable formulation and relevant exceptions.
- If consequence arguments conflict, separate juridical implications, causal forecasts, and value judgments; identify the legally permitted role of each.
- If an authority is final or binding, keep reasoned criticism distinct from competence to depart.
- If a counterexample works, revise the rule and rerun the original and comparison cases. Explain both the gain and the cost of the revision.

## Key Takeaways

The syllogism exposes the legal claim's structure; it does not supply its premises. Interpretation, proof, classification, and relevancy need different support. Consistency, coherence, universalizability, and consequences discipline disputed rulings. Finality, authority, and justified correctness remain distinct. The advisor uses this as an organizing framework, with specialist methods loaded for evidence, relations, precedent, and defeat.
