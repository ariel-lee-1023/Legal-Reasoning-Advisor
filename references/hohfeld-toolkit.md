# Hohfeld Toolkit — Some Fundamental Legal Conceptions as Applied in Judicial Reasoning

**Source:** Wesley Newcomb Hohfeld, *Some Fundamental Legal Conceptions as Applied in Judicial Reasoning*, 23 Yale Law Journal 16–59 (1913), supplied Markdown transcription. **Format**: md | **Pages**: 44 journal pages | **Sections**: 3 | **Depth**: study | **Type**: text

## Mental Model (read first)

A legal interest is an arrangement of relations among persons, not a physical thing or a single undifferentiated right. Identify the persons, conduct or legal change, and time before deciding which relation exists. The eight conceptions clarify what a proposition asserts; they do not establish that the law grants the asserted position. Hohfeld's opening problem is the analysis of trusts and equitable interests: treating all interests as either rights in rem or rights in personam obscures the different relations within them.

## Frameworks & Structure

### 1. Legal Conceptions Contrasted with Non-Legal Conceptions — pp. 20–25

**Legal relation / physical or mental fact.** A spoken offer, a person's intention, and delivery of a document may generate relations under an applicable rule. They are not themselves the resulting power, duty, or immunity. Ask separately what happened and what legal difference it made. A speaker's continuing willingness is not the same as the continued existence of an offeree's power of acceptance.

**Property.** The word can denote an object or the aggregate of legal relations concerning it. Moving from “this is my property” to “I can prevent this use” skips the relation's content, counterpart, exceptions, and source. Hohfeld criticizes the physical picture behind the corporeal/incorporeal distinction: an interest concerning a physical object is still a legal relation. This analytical criticism does not abolish the doctrinal categories or prove every property interest identical.

**Transfer.** Moving a watch is distinguishable from extinguishing one person's legal positions and creating corresponding positions in another. A transfer may involve changes in claims, privileges, powers, and immunities on different conditions. Physical delivery may be necessary, sufficient, evidential, or irrelevant depending on the rule; the object does not carry a universal legal bundle by itself.

**Trust and forum.** The article begins with rival accounts of equitable interests, including Coke's description of the use at Co. Litt. 272b. A lack of a common-law remedy is not a demonstration that equity creates no legal relations. Identify trustee, beneficiary, third party, forum, and the particular relation rather than deciding the whole dispute with “equity acts in personam.” Hohfeld announces further analysis; this 1913 installment does not supply the complete later treatment of in rem and in personam relations.

**License, capacity, possession, and title.** These words can name groups of operative facts rather than a fundamental relation. A license may be the permission-producing transaction; the privilege is one legal result. “Capacity” may identify age and other qualifying circumstances, while the resulting power concerns a specific legal change. Preserve the source's term and translate only the proposition actually at issue.

### 2. Operative Facts Contrasted with Evidential Facts — pp. 25–28

**Operative facts** (also constitutive, causal, or dispositive facts) are the facts which, under the applicable legal rules, create, extinguish, or alter legal relations. Their status depends on a rule. The act of acceptance cannot be treated as legally operative until the applicable formation conditions are identified.

**Affirmative and negative operative facts.** A legally effective transaction may depend on something happening and on a defeater not happening: acceptance plus absence of an effective prior revocation, for example. State the alleged absence and its evidential support separately. The conceptual importance of a negative fact does not decide who bears its procedural burden of proof.

**Evidential facts** provide a basis for inferring another fact, whether operative or itself evidential. An email log may support an inference of delivery; delivery may complete a legally significant act. Preserve the chain rather than treating evidence of a transaction as the transaction itself.

**The document across time.** In Hohfeld's January-contract example, the instrument's creation and delivery in January are operative circumstances. Inspection of the paper in later litigation is evidence of those earlier circumstances. Its present existence does not establish unchanged wording, timely delivery, authority, or legal effectiveness. The same object can have different functions at different times.

**Generic pleaded facts / particular historical facts.** A pleading may allege a transaction in a general form permitted by procedural rules. Real events have particular participants, dates, and conditions. Do not infer that every evidential detail must be pleaded, or that a sufficient pleading proves its operative allegations. Hohfeld's analytical distinction is not a substitute for the forum's pleading rules.

**Analytical payoff.** An apparent disagreement about a right may actually concern the evidence for a constitutive event. Conversely, agreement about all events may leave the governing rule or its interpretation disputed. Identify which uncertainty remains before requesting more facts.

### 3. Fundamental Jural Relations Contrasted with One Another — pp. 28–59

#### Correlatives and opposites

Correlatives express the same relation from its two sides. Opposites exclude each other for the same person, counterpart, content, time, and legal setting. Changing any of those coordinates may remove the apparent contradiction.

| Position of A | Correlative position of B | Opposite position of A | Exact question |
|---|---|---|---|
| Right, in the strict sense of claim | Duty | No-right | Must B do, or refrain from, a specified act as against A? |
| Privilege | No-right | Duty of contrary content | Is A under no duty to B not to perform this act? |
| Power | Liability | Disability | Can A bring about this specified legal change through a legally effective act? |
| Immunity | Disability | Liability | Does B lack power to bring about this specified change in A's position? |

**Content matters.** A privilege to enter is the absence of a duty not to enter. It can coexist with a duty to enter: a contractual obligation to visit one's own land does not make entry unprivileged. The opposite duty concerns abstention from the privileged act, not the performance of that act. A bilateral liberty to act or abstain requires examining both directions.

**Correlativity supplies no new legal premise.** Establishing A's claim that B refrain entails B's corresponding duty; merely naming A's privilege supplies no claim against B's interference. The apparatus checks the inference once its legal premise is established. It cannot create the premise or decide a policy controversy.

#### Rights and duties — pp. 30–32

A claim identifies another person's required conduct. Ask what performance, to whom, when due, under which conditions. “A can sue” may describe a procedural power or a complex remedial position rather than adequately stating the primary claim. A duty's breach and the availability of damages, an injunction, or a procedural vehicle require distinct propositions.

Do not translate every statutory occurrence of “right” into a claim. Hohfeld's judicial examples show the term used for powers, privileges, and immunities. Read the legal operation before selecting the technical label.

#### Privileges and no-rights — pp. 32–44

A privilege concerns the holder's conduct relative to another person's claim. It is neither universal permission nor guaranteed practical success. A no-right is the absence of the specified claim; it does not mean that the person has no legal protection of any kind.

**Salad illustration.** Permission from the owners to eat their salad can remove the eater's duty not to eat it without promising that the owners will refrain from holding the dish away. Conversely, ownership may support claims against others' eating while the owner contracts with one person not to eat it himself. The owner's privilege is then different against that promisee and against strangers. Permission, protection, and contractual restraint must be mapped separately.

**Quinn v. Leathem.** Hohfeld challenges the inference from a trader's liberty to deal with willing persons to a general duty in others not to interfere. The additional protection may be justified, but justice, policy, and governing law must supply it. This identifies a missing premise; it does not decide that all interference is lawful or refute every possible justification of the case's result.

**Mogul Steamship.** The loose language of competing “rights to trade” can conceal a dispute about whether competitors have a privilege to perform particular acts and whether those acts invade an established claim. Specify the conduct and correlatives before declaring a conflict. The terminology alone establishes neither a protected competitive method nor liability for it.

**Permission and protection travel separately.** A permission-giving act may also create contractual claims or powers, but that requires its own basis. Do not make the reverse mistake of assuming that a legal privilege can never accompany protection against interference.

#### Powers and liabilities — pp. 44–55

A power is a position under which legally significant facts within a person's volitional control can produce a change in legal relations. Identify the starting relation, authorized actor, operative act, conditions, resulting relation, and persons subjected to the change. A change occurring by death or another non-volitional event need not be an exercise of power by the person affected.

**Power / physical ability / privilege.** Hohfeld's owner who covenants not to convey to Z may retain the power to make an effective conveyance while owing the promisee a duty not to exercise it. Thus “prohibited” does not entail “ineffective.” Conversely, physical ability to sign or seize establishes no legal power. Determine both effectiveness and wrongfulness.

**Liability / duty.** Liability is subjection to a legal change, not necessarily fault, debt, loss, or an existing obligation to act. A person eligible to be called as a juror can be liable to the creation of a duty before the duty has arisen. Abandonment of property can subject others to changes that benefit them, including new privileges and opportunities to acquire title.

**Ordinary offer / option.** In Hohfeld's model, a qualifying offer creates the offeree's power of acceptance and the offeror's corresponding liability. An effective revocation extinguishes that power. A legally effective option can instead preserve the offeree's power during the specified period and remove the option-giver's power to revoke it. It need not be merely a promise whose breach produces damages after an effective revocation. Whether the actual option has this effect depends on its constitutive conditions.

**Escrow and conditions subsequent.** Performing the escrow conditions may exercise a power to acquire title. A grantor's so-called right of entry after a condition is broken can combine a privilege to enter and a power by entry to divest the grantee's estate. Until the necessary divesting act occurs, the estate can persist under liability. Do not equate the occurrence of the trigger with exercise of the resulting power.

**Agency and public calling.** An agent's authority concerns power to affect the principal's relations; a separate instruction concerns privilege or duty as between principal and agent. Hohfeld's innkeeper illustration treats the member of the public's qualifying application and tender as facts imposing a service duty, exposing the difference between a present liability and a presently owed duty. These historical examples are structural models, not rules about current agency or public accommodations.

#### Immunities and disabilities — pp. 55–58

An immunity concerns a specified person's lack of legal power over a specified relation. It does not guarantee factual security or freedom from all obligations. An owner may be immune from a stranger's attempted transfer but liable to a duly empowered sheriff's disposition, with some assets exempt and others subject to it.

**Phoenix Insurance and Morgan.** The tax-exemption discussions raise two questions: whether the grant creates an immunity from a taxing power, and whether that immunity transfers with the property or franchise. The conceptual classification alone does not resolve alienability. Hohfeld also records contrary treatment where the governing grant attached the benefit to the property. Preserve the terms and beneficiary of the particular exemption.

**Immunity / claim against interference.** A decision-maker may owe a duty not to exercise a power while retaining that power, or may lack the power altogether. These produce different consequences for an attempted act. A claim that an act is void requires a basis for disability or invalid exercise, not merely evidence of breach.

#### Lowest common denominators — pp. 58–59

The eight positions allow comparison across superficially different fields: an option, a conveyance, a trust, and an official act can each involve powers and liabilities. The comparison is structural, not an assertion that their conditions or remedies are identical. The closing text reserves fuller treatment of in rem and in personam relations; do not import later Hohfeld terminology as if this article developed it.

## Worked Example

Reconstruction of Hohfeld's option analysis, pp. 49–51. A makes B a qualifying offer to sell land. B has a power to accept; A is liable to the resulting contractual change. If A can effectively revoke, B is liable to loss of that power. Now assume a legally effective option preserves B's power for a specified period. A's later statement “I withdraw” does not necessarily extinguish it: a change of mind is a mental fact, while revocation is an operative legal effect. B may retain the power to accept and an immunity against A's attempted revocation. A promise not to revoke can also generate a claim/duty relation, but that alone does not tell us whether revocation is legally effective. The decisive inquiry is which relations the option's governing law and constitutive facts created, not whether everyday speech permits someone to withdraw an offer.

## Decision Rules & Judgment

- When a word changes from permission to protection, identify the independent source of the duty against interference.
- When an act is forbidden, test its legal effectiveness separately; power and privilege are independent positions.
- When a trigger occurs, identify whether a legal change is automatic or awaits exercise of a power.
- When a document is offered as proof, distinguish its historical operative role from its present evidential role.
- When a right is claimed “against everyone,” specify the relevant class and exceptions; a claim against B does not establish the same claim against C.
- When an exemption changes hands, investigate transfer conditions rather than inferring transferability from the label immunity.
- When all eight labels are available but no governing rule is supplied, present conditional mappings; terminology cannot establish the substantive law.
- When the question is already relationally clear, use only the distinction that changes the answer.

## Key Takeaways

1. Fix counterpart, content, and time before classifying a legal position.
2. Keep operative facts, their evidence, and the resulting relations separate.
3. Permission, protection, effective legal action, and freedom from another's power answer different questions.
4. Use Hohfeld to locate missing premises, not to manufacture doctrine or remedies.
